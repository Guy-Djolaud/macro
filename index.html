About

I study how macroeconomic shocks propagate through the Canadian economy and how monetary and fiscal policy should respond. My work combines Bayesian state-space models, time-series and panel econometrics, and large-scale confidential Statistics Canada microdata with structural macroeconomic models (DSGE, TANK) to analyse potential output, inflation and the Phillips curve, labour-market dynamics, and debt sustainability. I am fully bilingual in French and English.

Phillips Curve Flattening Mechanically Weakens NAIRU Identification
Submitted
Abstract

In the standard random-walk state-space model, the likelihood information about an interior NAIRU state relative to the smoothness precision supplied by the state equation is proportional to the squared Phillips curve slope. Canadian quarterly data from 1977 to 2026 show the mechanism is quantitatively severe: across the February 1991 inflation-targeting break this ratio falls by a factor of twenty-three, driven almost entirely by the slope. The credible interval for the unemployment gap then contains zero in 97 per cent of quarters, against 73 per cent before, and without a sign restriction the posterior probability of a negative slope falls from 0.99 to 0.78.

Immigration, Labour Dynamics, and Fiscal Sustainability
Working paper with Fatim Diabagate
Paper
Abstract

We examine the implications of fiscal policy for debt sustainability following an immigration shock. Using a DSGE model with life cycle, death risk, skills and labour efficiency, calibrated to Canada, we simulate the equilibrium effect of immigration on sustainable debt and show that the complementarity of immigrants' skills and their age profile have the largest impact on debt sustainability.

Labour Markets, Jobs, and Competition

Labour Market Power in Canada: Long-Term Trends and Cyclical Dynamics
Job market paper
PaperSlides
Abstract

The first time-series analysis of labour market concentration in Canada. National concentration declined gradually from 2001 to 2019, while local concentration rose by 14% during the Great Recession, 68% of which was driven by sectoral reallocation toward more concentrated industries. A calibrated TANK model with employer market power shows that fiscal expansions compress wage markdowns, raise the labour share, and markedly reduce consumption and income inequality between workers and capitalists.
